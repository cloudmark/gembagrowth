One of the quickest ways for a manager to become ineffective is to assume that everyone is motivated by the same things.

We've all met leaders who treat every employee exactly the same.

Every conversation follows the same formula.

Every meeting sounds identical.

Every problem is approached in the same way.

At first glance, this appears fair and consistent.

In reality, it often produces frustration.

Why?

Because people are different.

Some employees want detailed information before making a decision.

Others want to talk ideas through and build relationships.

Others simply want to know the objective and get moving.

Chris Voss explores this concept in *Never Split the Difference* by identifying three broad communication styles: **Analysts, Accommodators, and Assertives**. While no individual fits perfectly into any single category, understanding these tendencies can dramatically improve a manager's ability to communicate, influence, and lead effectively.[ ](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)[[Never Spli...Difference | PowerPoint]](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)

One of the most valuable lessons for leaders is this:

**Treating people equally does not necessarily mean treating them identically.**

### **Why Good Communication Sometimes Fails**

Many managers assume that if a message is clear, it should be understood.

Unfortunately, communication does not work that way.

People filter information through their own preferences, experiences, and priorities.

Imagine a manager announcing a major improvement project.

One employee immediately asks for data, timelines, and risk assessments.

Another wants to understand how the change will affect team relationships.

A third wants a simple overview and an implementation date.

The manager delivered one message.

Three completely different reactions followed.

The issue wasn't communication quality.

The issue was communication style.

The most effective leaders recognise these differences and adapt accordingly.

### **The Analyst**

Analysts are often methodical, detail-oriented, and highly rational in their approach.

According to Voss, analysts value information, logic, and accuracy. They generally prefer to work towards the best solution rather than the fastest one. They are often thoughtful, reserved, and highly sensitive to preparation and detail.[ ](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)[[Never Spli...Difference | PowerPoint]](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)

Every organisation has analysts.

They're often found in engineering, planning, quality, finance, procurement, and technical roles, although they can appear anywhere.

When communicating with analysts, leaders often make one critical mistake:

They rush.

An analyst who is pressured into making a quick decision may become uncomfortable.

The lack of information feels risky.

The lack of detail creates uncertainty.

The more pressure applied, the more resistance can emerge.

#### **Managing an Analyst**

When working with analysts:

- Be prepared.
- Bring data.
- Explain your rationale.
- Allow time for consideration.
- Avoid forcing immediate answers.

For example:

A manager announces a machine relocation project.

An analyst's first question might be:

*"What impact will this have on overall throughput?"*

That is not resistance.

That is how they think.

The worst response is:

*"Don't worry about the details."*

The better response is:

*"Let's review the data and work through it together."*

Analysts gain confidence through evidence.

Great leaders recognise this and adjust accordingly.

### **The Accommodator**

Accommodators are relationship-focused individuals.

They value collaboration, trust, and communication.

According to Voss, accommodators often enjoy discussion and relationship-building. They generally prefer cooperation and will frequently avoid unnecessary conflict.[ ](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)[[Never Spli...Difference | PowerPoint]](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)

Many supervisors, team leaders, and customer-facing staff naturally display accommodator tendencies.

These individuals often want to understand how decisions affect people.

While analysts ask:

*"Is this the right decision?"*

Accommodators often ask:

*"How will this impact the team?"*

#### **Managing an Accommodator**

The biggest mistake managers make with accommodators is becoming overly transactional.

A leader may focus entirely on targets, outputs, and deadlines while neglecting the human side of the discussion.

The accommodator can quickly feel disconnected.

When working with accommodators:

- Build rapport first.
- Encourage discussion.
- Allow time for questions.
- Recognise contributions.
- Explain the people impact of decisions.

For example, during a significant organisational change, an accommodator may be less concerned about technical implementation and more concerned about team morale.

Ignoring these concerns risks losing engagement.

Addressing them often creates strong support and commitment.

### **The Assertive**

Assertives approach situations differently again.

They tend to focus on results, speed, and achievement.

According to Voss, assertive personalities often view time as their most valuable resource. They are direct, decisive, and generally prefer action over prolonged discussion.[ ](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)[[Never Spli...Difference | PowerPoint]](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)

Every workplace has them.

Production managers.

Project leaders.

Senior executives.

High-performing supervisors.

Assertives often thrive in fast-paced environments where results matter.

The challenge is that assertives can sometimes appear impatient or dismissive when discussions become too detailed.

#### **Managing an Assertive**

When dealing with assertives:

- Get to the point quickly.
- Focus on outcomes.
- Avoid unnecessary detail.
- Demonstrate confidence.
- Be concise and clear.

Imagine presenting an improvement proposal.

An analyst might want a twenty-minute explanation.

An assertive may want the summary first:

*"What problem are we solving, how much improvement will we achieve, and when can we start?"*

Neither approach is wrong.

They're simply different.

Great leaders recognise the distinction.

### **The Leadership Mistake We All Make**

The biggest communication mistake most managers make is assuming others think the way they do.

This is particularly common among technically strong leaders.

An analytical manager often communicates analytically.

An assertive manager often communicates assertively.

An accommodator often focuses heavily on relationships.

The problem is that teams are rarely made up of identical personalities.

What motivates one employee may frustrate another.

What reassures one individual may overwhelm somebody else.

What feels efficient to one person may feel rushed to another.

Leadership becomes significantly easier once we stop expecting people to think like us.

### **A Practical Shop Floor Example**

Imagine a new production planning process is being introduced.

An analyst on the team asks for performance data and implementation risks.

An accommodator wants to understand how the change will affect shift teams.

An assertive wants to know when the new process will go live and what results are expected.

A manager who uses only one communication style may connect with one of those individuals.

A manager who adapts their approach can connect with all three.

The message remains the same.

The delivery changes.

This is not manipulation.

It is effective communication.

### **The Connection to Continuous Improvement**

Continuous improvement relies on people.

People solve problems.

People generate ideas.

People implement change.

The more effectively leaders communicate with different personality types, the more successful improvement efforts become.

Projects gain buy-in more quickly.

Resistance decreases.

Relationships strengthen.

Trust increases.

Most importantly, individuals feel understood.

And as we've seen throughout many of the principles in *Never Split the Difference*, people become more cooperative when they feel understood.[ ](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)[[Never Spli...Difference | PowerPoint]](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)

### **Final Thought**

Great managers do not have one leadership style.

They have one objective and multiple ways of achieving it.

Chris Voss's Analyst, Accommodator, and Assertive framework reminds us that effective communication is not about saying the same thing to everyone. It is about helping different people understand the message in a way that resonates with them.[ ](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)[[Never Spli...Difference | PowerPoint]](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)

The next time you're struggling to gain buy-in, remember this:

The problem may not be the message.

It may simply be the method.

Because leadership is not just about communicating clearly.

It's about communicating in a way that the other person can truly hear.
