Not long ago, I found myself in a conversation that many will recognise.

A process change was being discussed, and despite what felt like a perfectly logical explanation, one individual remained unconvinced. As the discussion continued, I found myself doing what many managers instinctively do: explaining more. More detail. More data. More justification.

Eventually the individual looked at me and said:

*"You're right."*

Years ago, I would have considered that a successful outcome. The discussion was over, agreement had apparently been reached, and we could move forward.  Except, I have learnt that those two words can be some of the most misleading words in leadership.

Over the years I've lost count of the number of times somebody has told me I was right, only for the real disagreement to surface later. Sometimes the plan wasn't followed. Sometimes concerns appeared weeks after the decision had been made. Occasionally the conversation would resurface in another meeting and it would become obvious that whilst the person had accepted my explanation, they had never truly accepted the conclusion.

What they had really been saying was:

*"I understand what you're saying."  *Or perhaps:  *"I understand your position." *What they weren't saying was: *"I share your view of the problem." *That distinction took me far longer to understand than it should have.

As managers, we're often taught to communicate clearly, be decisive, and provide direction. All of those things are important. The danger comes when we start believing that the objective of every conversation is agreement. We become focused on convincing people that our solution is correct instead of making sure we fully understand the problem they're experiencing.

And that is where I think the difference between *"You're right"* and *"That's right"* becomes incredibly important.

The first centres on the person.  The second centres on understanding.  One is about me.  The other is about the issue.  One says, "Your argument makes sense."  The other says, "We are now seeing the same reality."  For me, that is one of the most important differences in leadership.  Because the objective was never for people to think I was right.  The objective was for the team to get it right.
