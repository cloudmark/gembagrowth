One of the biggest surprises in my leadership journey has been discovering how often emotions drive behaviour.

As managers, we like to believe we make decisions based on facts. We look at data, analyse trends, track KPIs, and build business cases. Yet when I think honestly about many of the difficult conversations I've experienced over the years, the facts were rarely the thing causing the problem.

The emotion behind the facts usually was.

In many ways, we're all like that. Most of us know exactly what we should do. We know we should exercise more, eat better, worry less, and be more patient. Yet we don't always do those things. Logic may tell us one thing, while emotion pushes us in another direction.

I've certainly seen this in myself.

Earlier in my career, I would often take challenging conversations very personally. If somebody became defensive, frustrated, or confrontational, I would naturally react to the behaviour in front of me rather than considering what might be driving it. As a result, I would sometimes match their emotional state instead of helping to lower it.

One particular incident stands out.

A colleague came into my office to discuss an issue that had been causing tension. As the conversation progressed, they became increasingly defensive. I became frustrated in return. Before long, what started as a discussion had become an argument, and they eventually stormed out of my office.

At the time, I convinced myself the problem was their attitude.

Looking back, I realised I had completely missed what was actually happening.

Later, I learned that much of their frustration had very little to do with me. They had been dealing with pressures and challenges that had been building for some time, and our conversation had simply become the point where those emotions surfaced.

What I interpreted as resistance was actually frustration.  What I interpreted as confrontation was someone struggling under pressure.

The difficult lesson for me was realising that I had responded to the behaviour rather than the emotion behind it.

Years later, I came across the concept of **labelling**. The concept is not rocket science and has been used in gestalt psychology and emotional intelligence for many many years.  The idea is remarkably simple. Instead of immediately reacting, solving, defending, or persuading, you pause and identify what emotion may be present.

*"It sounds like you're frustrated."*

*"It seems like this situation has been weighing on you."*

*"It looks like there may be some concerns we haven't discussed yet."*

While Chris Voss popularised labelling through hostage negotiation and his book Never split the difference, the underlying principle appears throughout psychology. Carl Rogers called it reflecting feelings. Gestalt therapy encourages awareness of present emotions. Susan David describes emotions as data rather than directives. Neuroscientist Matthew Lieberman even demonstrated that putting feelings into words can reduce emotional reactivity in the brain. Different disciplines use different terminology, but the message is remarkably consistent: emotions lose much of their power when they are recognised and named.

When I first read about it, it seemed almost too simple to be effective.  Yet the more I practised it, the more powerful it became.  What I discovered was that people often don't need agreement first. They need understanding first.

That doesn't mean accepting poor behaviour. It doesn't mean lowering standards or avoiding accountability. It simply means acknowledging that another person is experiencing something.  One of the most important lessons I've learned is that understanding someone is not the same as agreeing with them. In fact, I've found that accountability conversations become easier when people feel understood. Once emotions begin to settle, people are far more willing to discuss facts, actions, and solutions.

I've always admired leaders who can sit in a challenging meeting, face criticism, and remain calm under pressure. For a long time, I assumed that ability came naturally to them.

I no longer think that's true. I suspect many of them are doing exactly what I've learned to do: slowing themselves down long enough to separate the emotion from the event. Even today, I'm not perfect at it.

There are still moments when I feel challenged, frustrated, or defensive. In those situations, I often have an internal conversation with myself before responding.

*"This is probably more about the situation than it is about you." - "Keep your cool." - "You've done your best with the information you had." - "Focus on understanding before responding."*

**That pause often makes all the difference.**

Studies in emotional intelligence and neuroscience suggest that when emotions are recognised and acknowledged, people are better able to regulate them and engage in more rational thinking. Put simply, people struggle to solve problems when they feel threatened, ignored, or misunderstood.

As leaders, we often rush towards solutions because that's what we're trained to do.  But sometimes the fastest route to solving a problem is not solving it immediately at all.  It's taking a moment to recognise what is happening beneath the surface.

The next time you find yourself in a difficult conversation, try mentally putting the behaviour into a box. Label it.  Not the person.  The emotion.

**Leadership isn't always about having the right answer. Sometimes it's about creating the conditions where people are ready to hear it.**
