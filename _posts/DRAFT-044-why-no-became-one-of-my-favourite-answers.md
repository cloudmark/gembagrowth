When I first started managing people, I had a fairly simple view of leadership. The manager's role was to make decisions, communicate them clearly, and ensure the team understood what needed to happen next. If people agreed with the direction, that felt like success. If they challenged it, that felt like a problem.

I suspect many new managers go through a similar phase.

There is something undeniably satisfying about presenting an idea and seeing a room full of people nod in agreement. It creates a sense of momentum. It reassures you that people understand your vision. If I am being completely honest, it also feeds the ego a little. After all, what manager doesn't enjoy feeling that they have a team fully aligned behind them?

The trouble is that I gradually discovered that agreement and commitment are not the same thing.

Early in my management career, I can remember countless occasions where I would walk out of a meeting feeling positive. We had discussed a new process, agreed a course of action, assigned responsibilities, and everyone around the table had responded with some version of "yes". The conversation felt productive. The plan seemed clear.  Then reality would intervene.

A few days later, little or nothing had happened. Sometimes the actions had not been completed. Sometimes they had not even been started. On more than one occasion, the exact opposite of what we had agreed was taking place.

At first, I found this frustrating. Eventually, it became fascinating.

I started asking myself a simple question: what did all those "yes" responses actually mean?

The more I observed people, the more I realised that "yes" can represent many different things. Sometimes it means genuine commitment. Sometimes it means "I understand". Sometimes it means "I don't want an argument". Sometimes it means "I'll think about it later". And occasionally it means "I disagree completely, but now isn't the time for that conversation".  It also could mean “I’m too disengaged to actually gave a thought about this”.

The danger for managers is assuming that every "yes" means buy-in.

In reality, some of the quietest resistance in organisations starts with people politely agreeing.

As I gained more experience, something unexpected happened. I stopped looking for agreement and started looking for engagement.

That does not mean I became attracted to conflict or encouraged people to be disruptive. We have all encountered individuals who reject every idea simply because they enjoy being the contrarian in the room. Every proposal is impossible. Every improvement is flawed. Every discussion begins and ends with a hard no.

Those conversations rarely move anything forward.

The people I value most are different.  They are the people who challenge constructively. When they say no, it comes attached to curiosity, thought, and an alternative perspective.

"Matthew, I don't think that approach will work, but what if we tackled it this way?"

"I'm not convinced we can achieve that timeline, but if we change the sequence of activities we might still reach the objective."

"I understand what we're trying to achieve, but I think we're overlooking a risk."

Those are the moments I have learned to appreciate.

Not because they are comfortable, but because they are useful.

A reasoned "no" tells me that somebody has been paying attention. It tells me they have taken ownership of the discussion rather than simply sitting through it. Most importantly, it tells me they care enough about the outcome to challenge an idea before it becomes a problem.

In many ways, these conversations reflect one of the most important principles of effective organisations: better decisions emerge when people feel safe enough to disagree.

The concept of psychological safety has been discussed extensively in leadership literature, but its application is surprisingly simple. People need to feel they can raise concerns, challenge assumptions, and offer alternative viewpoints without being labelled negative, difficult, or resistant.

Unfortunately, organisations often create the opposite environment without realising it.

When leaders become defensive, objections disappear.  When every challenge is treated as a roadblock, people stop raising them.  When disagreement is punished, even subtly, teams learn that staying quiet is the safest option.

The result is not alignment.  The result is silence.  And silence can be dangerous.

Some of the biggest mistakes I have seen in business were visible to somebody long before they happened. The problem wasn't that the warning signs weren't there. The problem was that nobody felt comfortable enough to voice them, or somebody did voice them and wasn't truly heard.

That is why I have become increasingly cautious whenever a room immediately agrees with everything being proposed.

A room with no questions worries me.  A room with no concerns worries me.  A room with no alternative ideas worries me.  Because real engagement is rarely that tidy.

The best teams I have worked with weren't the teams that always agreed. They were the teams that challenged each other respectfully. They debated ideas. They tested assumptions. They exposed risks. They brought different perspectives to the table. Sometimes those discussions slowed down a decision in the short term, but almost always improved it in the long term.

As leaders, I don't believe our role is to collect as many "yes" responses as possible.

Our role is to create an environment where people feel comfortable enough to tell us when we are wrong, when they see a risk, or when they have a better idea.

When somebody says, "No, I don't think that will work", they may not be creating a problem.

They may be offering us an opportunity.  An opportunity to uncover a blind spot.  An opportunity to improve an idea.  An opportunity to arrive at a better outcome than the one we originally had in mind.  After years of managing teams, I've come to realise that my favourite conversations rarely begin with "yes".

The most valuable ones usually begin with:  *"No... but what if we did this instead?"*
