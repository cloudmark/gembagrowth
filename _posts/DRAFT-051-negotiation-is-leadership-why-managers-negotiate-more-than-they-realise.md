When people hear the word *negotiation*, they often think of contract discussions, salary reviews, supplier agreements, or high-stakes business deals.

Few managers would describe their daily role as that of a negotiator.

Yet if we take a closer look at leadership, negotiation is happening everywhere.

A manager negotiates priorities with senior leadership.

A supervisor negotiates commitment from a team.

A project leader negotiates resources from other departments.

A production manager negotiates deadlines with customers.

A coach negotiates behavioural change with an employee.

Leadership and negotiation are far more closely connected than many people realise.

In fact, one of the biggest lessons from Chris Voss's *Never Split the Difference* is that successful negotiation is ultimately about understanding people, building trust, uncovering motivations, and influencing outcomes. Those are not just negotiation skills. The more I reflect on the principles in the book, the more convinced I become that managers are negotiating every single day, whether they realise it or not.

### **Leadership Is About Influence**

One of the biggest misconceptions about leadership is that authority creates results.

Authority can create compliance.

It cannot always create commitment.

A manager can tell an employee what needs to happen.

A leader gains genuine buy-in for why it matters.

There is a significant difference.

Anyone can instruct.

The best leaders influence.

And influence sits at the heart of negotiation.

Think about a major change initiative.

A company introduces a new process designed to improve performance.

Management sends communications, provides training, and explains the benefits.

Yet some people embrace the change while others resist it.

Why?

Because leadership is not simply about transmitting information.

It is about helping people move from their current position to a new one.

That is negotiation in its purest form.

### **The Daily Negotiations We Never Notice**

Many leadership conversations are negotiations disguised as something else.

Consider how often you find yourself trying to:

- Gain support for an improvement project
- Resolve conflict between departments
- Influence behaviours
- Secure resources
- Encourage accountability
- Align competing priorities
- Build commitment to a target

None of these discussions involve a formal negotiating table.

Yet every one of them requires the same skills.

Understanding needs.

Managing emotions.

Exploring concerns.

Building trust.

Finding solutions.

The reality is that managers rarely struggle because they lack technical knowledge.

They struggle because people are involved.

And whenever people are involved, negotiation skills become leadership skills.

### **Why Facts Alone Rarely Work**

One of the recurring themes throughout *Never Split the Difference* is that people are emotional before they are rational. Our decisions are heavily influenced by feelings, perceptions, fears, and motivations.[ ](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)[[Never Spli...Difference | PowerPoint]](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)

This has enormous implications for leaders.

How often have you seen someone reject an idea that was logically correct?

How often have you witnessed resistance despite overwhelming evidence?

How often have you delivered a strong business case only to encounter hesitation?

Most managers have experienced all three.

The reason is simple.

People do not commit because the logic is perfect.

People commit when they understand, trust, and believe.

This is why leaders who focus exclusively on facts often find themselves frustrated.

The technical argument is only one part of the conversation.

The emotional argument is often more important.

### **A Leadership Example**

Imagine a supervisor attempting to introduce a new standard operating procedure.

The process has been developed carefully.

The benefits are obvious.

Quality performance will improve.

Risk will decrease.

Customers will benefit.

The supervisor explains all of this.

The team remains sceptical.

An inexperienced leader may continue explaining.

A more effective leader becomes curious.

They ask questions.

They listen.

They uncover concerns.

Perhaps employees are worried about increased workload.

Perhaps previous initiatives were poorly implemented.

Perhaps confidence in leadership is low.

Once those concerns are understood, meaningful progress becomes possible.

The negotiation was never about the procedure.

It was about trust.

Many leadership challenges are exactly the same.

The stated problem is rarely the real problem.

### **The Leadership Toolkit**

Looking back at the concepts explored throughout the book, it becomes clear that they form a surprisingly effective leadership framework.[ ](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)[[Never Spli...Difference | PowerPoint]](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)

Great leaders:

#### **Listen More Than They Speak**

They use techniques such as mirroring to gather information and encourage discussion.

They understand that assumptions are dangerous.

The goal is understanding before action.

#### **Use Tactical Empathy**

They acknowledge concerns without necessarily agreeing with them.

They seek to understand before attempting to influence.

#### **Label Emotions**

They recognise frustration, uncertainty, and resistance rather than ignoring them.

By doing so, they help people move from emotion to rational discussion.

#### **Welcome "No"**

They understand that objections contain valuable information.

Rather than forcing agreement, they explore concerns.

#### **Ask Better Questions**

They develop ownership by encouraging people to think through problems rather than providing every answer.

#### **Focus on Fairness**

They recognise that people judge decisions not only by outcomes, but by how those outcomes were achieved.

Each of these behaviours makes managers more effective.

Collectively, they create stronger leaders.

### **Leadership Is Not Winning**

One of the most important lessons I took from *Never Split the Difference* is that successful negotiation is not about winning.

The same is true of leadership.

Too many managers approach disagreement as a contest.

A challenge appears.

Positions emerge.

Somebody must be proven right.

Unfortunately, this approach often creates compliance at the expense of commitment.

Great leaders think differently.

Their objective is not winning an argument.

Their objective is solving a problem.

Sometimes that means changing their own perspective.

Sometimes it means adapting a proposal.

Sometimes it means learning something they did not know before.

Leadership, like negotiation, requires humility.

### **Building a Culture of Collaboration**

The strongest teams are not built through authority alone.

They are built through trust.

Trust grows when people feel:

- Heard
- Understood
- Respected
- Involved
- Treated fairly

This is why the principles remain so relevant outside hostage negotiations and commercial discussions.

At their core, they are principles about human interaction.

And leadership is fundamentally a human activity.

### **Final Thought**

When most people think about negotiation, they imagine a meeting room, a contract, or a commercial agreement.

In reality, negotiation happens every day on the shop floor, in project meetings, during coaching conversations, and throughout every change initiative.

Leadership is the art of influencing people towards a better outcome.

Negotiation is the art of influencing people towards a better outcome.

The overlap is impossible to ignore.
