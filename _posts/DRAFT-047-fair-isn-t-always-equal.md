A few years ago, during a town hall discussion, an employee accused me of not being fair.  It was one of the more interesting leadership conversations I've had.  My response surprised a few people because I didn't disagree.

In fact, I told them they were probably right.  I explained that I absolutely gave more opportunities, more responsibility, and more support to certain individuals within the team. The people who consistently stepped forward, took ownership, supported improvement initiatives, volunteered when extra help was needed, and demonstrated a willingness to grow were often the same people I invested most of my time in.

At first glance, that doesn't sound particularly fair.  After all, shouldn't everybody be treated the same?

The longer I have managed people, however, the more I've come to realise that fairness and equality are not the same thing.  In many organisations, the two terms are used interchangeably. We assume that if everyone receives exactly the same treatment, the situation must be fair. Yet leadership is rarely that simple.

### **The Problem With Equal**

Most people naturally associate fairness with equality.  Equal workload.  Equal opportunity.  Equal  recognition.  Equal attention.  Equal reward.  The difficulty is that teams are not made up of equal circumstances.

They are made up of different individuals with different abilities, different aspirations, different commitments, and different levels of contribution.  Treating everybody identically may feel fair on paper, but it can create unfair outcomes in practice.

One of the most common examples is something many high performers quietly joke about:  *The reward for hard work is more work.  *Most managers will recognise this pattern immediately.  A difficult project appears.  A key customer issue emerges.  An urgent task needs ownership.  Without thinking too much about it, we often turn to the person we trust most to deliver.

Initially, this can be positive. High performers are often motivated by responsibility and enjoy being trusted with important work.  Eventually, however, a problem can emerge.  The individual carrying the additional responsibility starts looking around and asking a perfectly reasonable question:

*"Why am I always the one being asked?"  *At that point, what felt fair to the manager can start feeling unfair to the employee.

### **The Tugboat Lesson**

I've always found the image of tugboats moving a ship surprisingly useful when thinking about fairness.

Imagine several tugboats guiding a large vessel into port.  If every tugboat applied exactly the same force in exactly the same direction, there is a good chance the ship would struggle to reach its destination efficiently. Depending on where the boats were positioned, it might even drift off course.

Each tugboat has a different role.  Each applies a different amount of force.  Each contributes differently to the overall outcome.  The objective isn't equal effort.  The objective is achieving the result.  Leadership works much the same way.

There are times when some individuals need additional support.  There are times when some individuals take on greater responsibility.  There are times when one person contributes significantly more effort than another because the situation demands it.  That is not necessarily unfair.  In fact, it may be exactly what fairness requires.  The challenge is helping people understand the difference.

### **Why Fairness Is About Perception**

One lesson I've learned repeatedly is that fairness isn't measured on a spreadsheet.  It's experienced.  Two employees can look at exactly the same decision and arrive at completely different conclusions.  One sees fairness.  The other sees favouritism.  One sees recognition.  The other sees inequality.

The reality often matters less than the perception.  This is why communication becomes so important.  I've found that people are surprisingly accepting of decisions they don't personally benefit from when they understand the reasoning behind them.

What they struggle with is inconsistency.  Or secrecy.  Or the feeling that different rules apply to different people.  The issue is rarely the outcome alone.  It's whether the process feels fair.

### **Where Leaders Get It Wrong**

Many managers spend considerable time making decisions and very little time explaining them.  They assume the logic is obvious.  Unfortunately, what is obvious to the decision-maker is often invisible to everybody else.

I've seen leaders allocate overtime, development opportunities, projects, training, and promotions with perfectly good intentions, only to find resentment building within the team.  Not because the decision itself was unreasonable.  Because the reasoning was never shared.  People fill gaps in information with assumptions.  And those assumptions are rarely generous.

### **Fair Doesn't Mean Everybody Gets the Same**

Stephen Covey's principle of Think Win-Win has always resonated with me because it focuses on outcomes rather than simplistic equality. A successful team is not one where everyone receives identical treatment.

It's one where people feel respected, valued, and given the opportunity to contribute meaningfully.   Sometimes that means investing more heavily in somebody who is ready for greater responsibility.  Sometimes it means providing additional support to somebody who is struggling.  Sometimes it means recognising exceptional contribution rather than pretending every contribution is identical.

None of those situations are equal.  All of them can be fair.
