If I am honest, statistics did not come naturally to me. During my engineering degree, I struggled—and failed both mathematics and statistics. I am not kidding. At the time, the subjects felt abstract, overly theoretical, and disconnected from anything real. The formulas seemed like things to memorise, not ideas to understand.

Then something changed.

A couple of years later, while studying for my MBA, I took a module in qualitative and quantitative analysis. I expected more of the same. Instead, I experienced something completely different. Concepts that had once felt complex—standard deviation, variation, distributions—started to make sense. Not because the maths had changed, but because the *context* had. Credit goes to the lecturer who sparked this interest. He knows who he is!

For the first time, I could relate the concepts to real situations. I was no longer solving equations in isolation; I was making sense of variation in processes, uncertainty in decisions, and patterns in behaviour. Statistics stopped being just a subject and became a tool.

That shift was significant.

Working in manufacturing reinforced this even further. I began to see how statistical thinking could support better decisions. A change in a measurement was not always a real improvement. One small sample did not define a process. Variation was not something to eliminate entirely, but something to understand and manage. These ideas can be mathematically simple, yet operationally powerful.

What struck me most over time was how widely these concepts apply.

Statistics is often associated with high-volume manufacturing or quality engineering. It is easy to assume it belongs only in spreadsheets, dashboards, and technical reports. In reality, it is everywhere.

Consider a fast-food chain deciding how many burgers to prepare at different times of day. That involves forecasting demand and managing uncertainty. A bank trying to understand queues and waiting times is dealing with variation and distributions. Schools use data to examine admissions and performance. Surveys that measure customer satisfaction rely on sampling and inference.

Beyond that, the reach is even broader.

Medicine uses statistics to evaluate treatments and assess risk. Psychology uses it to study behaviour. Sports teams analyse performance data. Travel companies adjust prices in response to demand. Even everyday choices, such as when to leave home to avoid traffic, involve an intuitive sense of variability and probability.

The common thread is simple. Statistics helps us make sense of uncertainty.

Looking back, the turning point was not that I suddenly became better at maths. It was that I could see the relevance. Once I understood how these ideas applied to real problems, everything became more accessible. The formulas were no longer barriers; they were expressions of concepts I could connect to.

There is a wider lesson in this.

Many people are intimidated by statistics and data analysis. They have a reputation for being difficult and overly technical. But sometimes the obstacle is not the complexity of the subject; it is the lack of context. When we connect the ideas to something familiar, they become far more approachable.

My experience reminds me that understanding does not always come immediately. Sometimes it takes the right moment, the right context, or the right problem to bring an idea into focus.

When that happens, what once felt difficult can become not only manageable, but genuinely interesting. For me, statistics changed when I stopped seeing it as a set of formulas and started seeing it as a way to ask better questions about the world around me.
