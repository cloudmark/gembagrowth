For most of my career, I believed the reward for hard work was more hard work. That mindset served me well. I built my career by solving problems—learning processes, challenging assumptions, and finding answers. Every problem solved was an opportunity to add value.

Over time, that approach helped me progress. The more problems I solved, the more responsibility I was given. What nobody told me was that leadership would eventually require a different skill set.

The higher I moved, the less my job became about solving problems myself and the more it became about helping others solve theirs. **That transition was harder than I expected.** My instinct was to jump in. When someone brought me a problem, I immediately began thinking of solutions. After all, that behaviour had been rewarded throughout my career.

> When a manager becomes the person with all the answers, the team can stop looking for their own.

Over time, I realised my most effective mentors approached leadership differently. They rarely gave me answers. Instead, they gave me space.

When I came to them with an issue, they did not interrogate or instruct. They would respond with something deceptively simple:

**“Really?”**

**“Is that so?”**

**“Numbers are down this week?”**

At first, I found it frustrating. I wanted guidance, and they seemed to be giving me my own words back. But then something interesting happened: I kept talking. I explained more, added details, and connected dots I had not seen before. Somewhere in that conversation, I often discovered the answer myself.

Years later, after reading Chris Voss’s *Never Split the Difference*, I learned there was a name for one of the techniques my mentors used: **mirroring**.

Mirroring is simple. You repeat a key word or short phrase someone has just said, then give them room to continue. Used with genuine curiosity, it can signal that you are listening and invite the other person to say more. The aim is not to mimic someone or steer them toward a response; it is to understand what they mean before rushing to offer your own interpretation.

Looking back, my mentors were not just helping me solve individual problems; they were helping me learn how to think. Rather than becoming a crutch, they encouraged me to investigate, understand, and develop confidence in my own judgement. In many ways, they were preparing me for leadership.

Today, I find myself in their position. When a team member brings me a problem, my instinct is still to solve it—but I have learned that the greatest value I can provide is often not the answer. It is helping them explore the question behind the question.

Sometimes I simply reflect what I have heard:

**“The process isn’t working?”**

**“The deliveries are late?”**

**“You’re concerned about the forecast?”**

Then I wait.

What follows is often more valuable than the advice I was preparing to give. People unpack the situation themselves. The real issue begins to emerge. Assumptions are challenged, possible solutions take shape, and ownership stays with the person closest to the problem.

That does not mean a leader should never offer direction. Some situations call for a clear decision, immediate action, or direct instruction. But when there is room to coach, listening before solving can help people build capability instead of simply handing the problem upwards.

> Leadership is about creating an environment where others become more capable of finding answers themselves—building a team confident enough to think, challenge, and solve problems.

Some of the most impactful conversations I have had, as both a mentee and a manager, contained very few words.

Just a mirror. A pause.

And the trust that the other person may be capable of figuring out more than they realise.
