For most of my career, I believed the reward for hard work was more hard work.

That sounds obvious, but it was a mindset that served me well. I built my career by solving problems. When something went wrong, I wanted to be the person who could fix it. I learned the processes, understood the products, challenged assumptions, and worked hard to find answers. Every problem solved was an opportunity to add value.

Over time, that approach helped me progress through the organisation. The more problems I solved, the more responsibility I was given.

What nobody told me was that leadership would eventually require a completely different skill set.

The higher I moved, the less my job became about solving problems myself and the more it became about helping others solve theirs.  That transition was harder than I expected.

My natural instinct was always to jump in. When somebody brought me a problem, I immediately began thinking of solutions. I wanted to help. I wanted to provide answers. After all, that behaviour had been rewarded throughout my career.

The challenge is that when a manager becomes the person with all the answers, the team often stops looking for their own.

Over the years, I began to realise that my most effective managers and mentors had approached leadership very differently. Looking back, I can see that they rarely gave me answers at all.

Instead, they gave me space.

When I approached them with an issue, they didn't interrogate me or tell me what to do next. They would often respond with something deceptively simple.

*"Really?"*

*"Is that so?"*

*"Numbers are down this week?"*

At the time, I sometimes found it frustrating. I was looking for guidance, and they seemed to be giving me my own words back. But then something interesting would happen.

I would continue talking. I would explain more. I would add details. I would start connecting dots that I had not connected before.

And often, somewhere in the middle of that conversation, I would discover the answer myself.

Years later, after reading Chris Voss's *Never Split the Difference*, I realised there was a name for what they were doing: **mirroring**.

Mirroring is remarkably simple. You repeat a key word or phrase someone has just said and allow them to continue.

It feels almost too simple to work. Yet it does.

The reason is that people want to be heard more than they want to be questioned. A mirrored response signals genuine interest without directing the conversation. Instead of leading someone toward your conclusion, it encourages them to explore their own thinking.

Looking back, I realise my mentors were not helping me solve problems. They were teaching me how to think.

They weren't interested in becoming a crutch that I would depend on every time I encountered a challenge. They wanted me to investigate, understand, and develop confidence in my own judgement.

In many ways, they were preparing me for leadership.

Today, I find myself in the same position they once occupied.

When a member of my team approaches me with a problem, my instinct is still to solve it. That instinct probably never disappears completely. However, I have learned that the greatest value I can provide is often not the answer.  It is the question behind the question.

Or sometimes, it is not even a question at all.

Sometimes it is simply:

*"The process isn't working?"*

*"The deliveries are late?"*

*"You're concerned about the forecast?"*

Then I wait.

What follows is often far more valuable than the advice I was preparing to give. People begin to unpack the situation themselves. The real issue emerges. Assumptions are challenged. Solutions start to form.

More importantly, ownership remains with the person closest to the problem. That is perhaps the biggest leadership lesson I have learned. Finding solutions will make you a valuable contributor. It may even earn you promotions. But continuously being the person with all the answers can create dependency.

**Leadership is different**. Leadership is creating an environment where other people become capable of finding the answers themselves.

The role of a manager is not to be the smartest person in the room. It is to build a room full of people who are confident enough to think, challenge, investigate and solve problems without waiting for permission.

Ironically, some of the most impactful conversations I have experienced as both a mentee and a manager have contained very few words at all.

Just a mirror.

A pause.

And the trust that the other person is capable of figuring out more than they realise.
