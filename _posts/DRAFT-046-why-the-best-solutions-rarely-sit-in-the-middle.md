Two people need a lemon.

Most of us, when asked to find a fair solution, would suggest cutting it in half. Each person receives half the lemon, the outcome appears balanced, and the disagreement is resolved. It feels reasonable because compromise is often associated with fairness.

The story becomes more interesting when someone asks a simple question:

*"What do you need the lemon for?"*

One person needs the zest. The other needs the juice.

Suddenly, the compromise no longer looks particularly effective. The person who needed the zest has little use for most of the fruit itself, while the person who needed the juice has no real need for the peel. By focusing solely on dividing the lemon equally, both people receive less of what they actually need than they could have received had they spent a little more time understanding each other's objectives.

I've thought about that story several times since first hearing it because it captures a mistake I see regularly in organisations. We often rush to negotiate a solution before we've properly understood the problem.

One of the earliest leadership lessons I learned was that when people disagree, they should meet in the middle. At face value, this sounds sensible. When departments have competing priorities, find a compromise. When stakeholders disagree, split the difference. When teams want different outcomes, look for the halfway point.

The advice is well intentioned, but increasingly I've come to believe that compromise is often overrated.

The issue is not that compromise is inherently wrong. Sometimes it is exactly the right answer. The issue is that compromise assumes the best solution lies somewhere between two positions. In reality, the best solution is often discovered only after we understand the interests that sit behind those positions.

A sales team may argue for shorter lead times while operations push for schedule stability. Viewed through the lens of positions, the situation appears straightforward. One side wants responsiveness, the other wants predictability. The obvious answer is to give each side a little of what it wants.

However, once you explore the underlying interests, the conversation changes. Sales may not actually care about lead times as an objective in themselves. What they want is higher customer satisfaction and improved competitiveness. Operations may not really be pursuing rigid schedules. Their goal may be efficiency, stability, and reliable execution. When viewed in this way, the disagreement is no longer about lead times at all. Both groups are trying to achieve positive customer outcomes while maintaining operational performance.

The same pattern appears repeatedly in manufacturing environments. Production teams and maintenance teams can appear to be pulling in opposite directions, with one wanting machines running and the other wanting downtime for preventative maintenance. The temptation is to negotiate over the number of hours allocated to each activity.

Yet when the discussion moves beyond positions, it becomes apparent that both teams are usually pursuing exactly the same objective: reliable output. Once that common objective is recognised, entirely different solutions become available, whether through predictive maintenance, condition monitoring, improved scheduling, or changes to asset strategy. The original argument was never really about downtime. Downtime was simply the proposed solution to a deeper need.

This is one of the reasons I think leaders can become overly attracted to compromise. Compromise creates the appearance of progress. Meetings conclude more quickly, disagreements become less visible, and everyone feels they have been heard. What compromise does not guarantee, however, is that the underlying issue has been solved.

I've seen major improvement initiatives gradually diluted in order to gain universal agreement. With each concession, opposition reduces and acceptance grows. Unfortunately, the benefits often reduce as well. The organisation achieves consensus but loses much of the value the change was intended to deliver. The result is an agreement that satisfies the process without significantly improving the outcome.

Over time, I've found that the most productive conversations start with a different set of questions. Rather than asking where the middle ground lies, I try to understand what success looks like for each party involved. What are they really trying to achieve? What risk are they trying to avoid? What outcome matters most to them?

Those questions frequently reveal that people are much more aligned than they initially appear. Their proposed solutions may differ significantly, but their objectives are often remarkably similar.

Looking back, I think the greatest lesson from the lemon story is not about negotiation at all. It's about curiosity. The breakthrough only occurs because someone asks one additional question. Without it, both parties settle for an outcome that appears fair but is objectively inferior.

As a younger manager, I believed part of my role was helping people find middle ground. Today, I think my role is helping people discover common ground.

They are not the same thing.

**Middle ground is where opposing positions meet. Common ground is where shared interests begin.**

Sometimes the best answer does sit in the middle. More often, however, the best answer emerges only when people stop defending what they want and start explaining why they want it.

The solution was never really about the lemon.

It was about understanding the need behind the request.
