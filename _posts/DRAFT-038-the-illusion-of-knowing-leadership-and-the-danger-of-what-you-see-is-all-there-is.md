One of the quieter pressures of management is the expectation to know.

Not just to guide or support, but to have answers. To make decisions quickly. To speak with clarity, even when the situation itself is not entirely clear. Over time, that expectation shapes how we behave. We become more decisive, more confident, more willing to trust our judgment.

On the surface, these are all positive traits. But there is a risk underneath them.

I have been reflecting on this while reading *Thinking, Fast and Slow* by Daniel Kahneman, particularly his concept of the illusion of validity. The more I think about it, the more I recognise how easily it takes hold in leadership roles. If I am being honest, I have been a culprit of it myself.

Kahneman’s idea is simple but uncomfortable. We feel confident in our judgments even when they are based on limited or unreliable information. That confidence does not come from strong evidence. It comes from coherence. If the story in our head makes sense, we tend to believe it is true.

We build conclusions from what is in front of us and behave as if it is the full picture. We rarely stop to consider what might be missing. Kahneman calls this “What You See Is All There Is”.

In management, the picture is almost always incomplete.

One example he shares has always stayed with me. Israeli military officers were asked to evaluate recruits and predict who would go on to become effective leaders. They observed candidates in exercises, formed strong impressions, and made confident judgments about who had leadership potential.

When those predictions were later compared to actual outcomes, the results were sobering. Their assessments were only slightly better than chance. Yet their confidence did not change. Even when faced with evidence that their judgments were unreliable, they continued to trust them.

There is something very familiar in that.

In the workplace, we do this constantly, just in more subtle ways. We meet someone once and feel we understand them. We leave an interview convinced a candidate will succeed. We see a team member in a handful of situations and form a lasting view of their capability.

Those judgments feel right. They feel complete.

But they are often built on fragments. A few interactions, a moment in time, limited context. From that, we create a full story without realising we are filling in the gaps.

Earlier in my career, I can see where I leaned into that confidence more than I should have. There is an unspoken belief that leadership means having clarity, that hesitation looks like weakness. So you form views quickly and trust your instinct.

Sometimes that works. But other times, what you are trusting is not deep insight. It is a well-constructed story built from incomplete information.

What has changed for me is not a loss of confidence, but a different relationship with it. I am more aware that the feeling of certainty does not mean I am right. It often just means the explanation in my head hangs together well.

That is not the same as being accurate.

It has made me more comfortable pausing before drawing conclusions and questioning whether I am reacting to evidence or just a narrative that happens to make sense. It also means acknowledging gaps. There are things I do not know, perspectives I have not heard, data I have not yet seen.

That does not make decisions easier, but it makes them more grounded.

It also changes how I look back on decisions. With hindsight, everything looks obvious. Everyone becomes a genius after the fact. But we do not lead with hindsight. We lead with the information we have at the time.

Kierkegaard captured this well: life can only be understood backwards, but it must be lived forwards.

We often associate strong leadership with decisiveness and certainty. But there is another, less visible side to it. Being able to hold uncertainty without rushing to resolve it. Being willing to say, “we do not have enough information yet”.

That does not always feel comfortable. It does not always fit expectations. But in many cases, it is the more honest and disciplined response.

Kahneman does not suggest we can eliminate these biases. We are wired to trust coherent stories and judgments that feel right. What we can do is become more aware of that tendency.

We can slow down when it matters. We can ask what might be missing. We can separate confidence from evidence.

For me, one of the most important shifts has been letting go of the need to appear as if I know everything. Being open and saying, “the interview went well, but we will only know once the person is in the role”.

It sounds simple, but it challenges the ego. Our instinct is to say, “I knew it”. In reality, we rarely do. And that is fine. It is unrealistic to expect certainty in complex environments.

As Steve Jobs put it, you cannot connect the dots looking forward, only backwards.

Leadership is not about knowing everything. It is about navigating uncertainty with honesty and clarity, and recognising that sometimes the biggest risk is not what we do not know.

It is believing, too quickly, that we already do.
