For most of my younger years, I studied piano. Fourteen years of scales, arpeggios, sight reading, examinations, and countless hours of practice. Looking back, I realise that some of the most important lessons I learned had very little to do with music itself.

One lesson in particular has stayed with me throughout my career.

*"Slow down."*

My piano teacher, Ms. Portelli, would say it repeatedly…(very repeatedly).  Whenever I encountered a difficult passage, particularly one that needed to be played at speed, my instinct was always the same. I would try to play it faster.  The logic seemed reasonable. If the piece needed to be fast, surely the best way to learn it was to practice it fast. The result was almost always a mess.

My fingers would rush ahead of themselves. Notes would blur together. Timing would become inconsistent. What should have sounded crisp and controlled became hurried and inaccurate.  Each time, Ms. Portelli would stop me and deliver the same instruction:

*"Slow down." - This would have been accompanied with the part where the book would meet my face, and a nice big circle drawn around the notes in pencil!*

What I eventually learned was that speed was never the objective.  Mastery was.

The only way to truly learn those passages was to play them slowly, deliberately and with intention. Every note had to be placed correctly. Every movement had to be understood. Every sequence had to become embedded in muscle memory.  Only when the foundations were solid could speed be introduced.

Ironically, the fastest way to play a passage well was first to play it slowly.  Over time, something remarkable happened. The thinking disappeared. My fingers simply knew where to go. The countless repetitions had built patterns that became automatic. What once required intense concentration became effortless execution.  *The book would not come to meet my face!*

Years later, I realised the same principle applies far beyond music.

### **The Difference Between Training and Panic**

In management, we often face situations that feel urgent.  A customer order is at risk; A supplier has failed; A machine has gone down; A project is slipping behind schedule; The natural response is often identical to my younger self sitting at the piano.

Speed up. Talk faster. Think faster. Act faster.

Yet the best leaders I have worked with rarely behave this way.

Instead, **they slow things down**. Not because they are passive or indecisive. Quite the opposite. They understand that clarity comes before action. When pressure rises, many people move into a state of reaction. They jump between conversations, search for answers, defend positions and attempt to solve everything simultaneously.

In reality, very little effective problem solving occurs. Activity increases. Progress decreases.

The situation becomes the organisational equivalent of trying to play a difficult piano passage before you've truly learned it.

### **When Experience Becomes Second Nature**

One of the most fascinating things to observe is how experienced professionals perform under pressure. Watch an experienced paramedic responding to an emergency. Observe a pilot handling an unexpected event. Spend time with a seasoned engineer diagnosing a critical equipment failure. They often appear surprisingly calm.

The reason is not that they care less. It is that training has created familiarity. They are not inventing their response in the moment. They are executing well-practised patterns. Just as a pianist no longer consciously thinks about every individual note, experienced professionals rely on knowledge and training that have been deeply embedded through repetition and practice.

The situation may be new. The principles are not. This allows them to move deliberately while everyone else is rushing.

### **Sharpening the Saw**

Stephen Covey popularised the phrase "sharpen the saw", and I have always thought it captures this concept perfectly. When faced with a problem, our instinct is often to work harder, push faster and apply more effort.

Sometimes the better answer is to stop. **Take a breath. Step back. Understand the situation. Gather facts. Challenge assumptions. Create clarity.**

It feels slower in the moment, but it usually accelerates progress. A dull saw swung with greater force rarely cuts faster. A sharp saw does. The same applies to decision making.

### **What Is Really Happening?**

There is another reason why slowing down matters. When pressure rises, emotions rise with it. We become concerned about missed targets, dissatisfied customers, project delays, reputational damage and personal accountability. Our focus narrows. We become reactive. Curiosity gives way to defensiveness. The quality of our thinking deteriorates precisely when we need it most.

This is why one of the most powerful questions a leader can ask is:

**"What do we know for certain?"**

It forces a shift from emotion to evidence. From assumptions to facts. From reaction to analysis. The question creates the same pause that my piano teacher created every time she told me to slow down.

It interrupts the rush and allows quality thinking to return.

### **The Leadership Lesson**

The older I get, the more I appreciate what Ms. Portelli was trying to teach me. She was not teaching me how to play faster. She was teaching me how to perform better. Speed would come naturally once competence, confidence and consistency were established. The same lesson applies in leadership.

The leaders I admire most are rarely the loudest people in the room. They rarely create urgency through volume or emotion. Instead, they bring calm. They create space for thinking. They slow conversations down when everyone else wants to speed them up.

Most importantly, they trust the preparation that has taken place before the crisis arrives.

Because when training is strong, values are clear and principles are understood, execution becomes second nature.
