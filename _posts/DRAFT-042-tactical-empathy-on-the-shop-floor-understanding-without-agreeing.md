One of the biggest misconceptions in leadership is the belief that empathy makes managers soft.

Many leaders worry that if they spend too much time listening to people's concerns, they'll lose authority. They'll be seen as indecisive. They'll compromise standards. They'll allow people to talk their way out of accountability.

A few years ago, I learned that the opposite is often true.

The lesson came during a manufacturing change that, on paper, should have been straightforward.  New regulations required us to move from leaded solder to lead-free solder across our production processes. There wasn't really a decision to make. The regulations had changed, and we had to comply.

The objective seemed simple enough: implement the change, maintain quality, keep production moving, and minimise disruption.  Then reality arrived.

I remember walking onto the shop floor one morning and being greeted by what felt like a wall of frustration.

Quality were complaining about poor soldering standards.  Operators were blaming Engineering for introducing a process that clearly wasn't working.  Engineering were becoming increasingly defensive because, from their perspective, they were simply implementing a regulatory requirement.

Nobody seemed happy.  Every conversation sounded like an argument.

*"It doesn't flow properly."; "It has no shine."; "It looks terrible."; "How are we supposed to work with this?"; "Whose bright idea was this?"; "You can't just switch over overnight."*

At the time, part of me wanted to respond the way many managers are tempted to respond.

"Guys, we have no choice. These are the regulations. We just need to get on with it."

I suspect many of us have had moments like that. The decision is made. The direction is clear. The logic is obvious. Why keep debating it? But for whatever reason, I didn't respond that way. Instead, I sat down next to two operators and listened.  Not listening so I could explain why they were wrong.  Not listening so I could defend the decision.  **Just listening.**

And something interesting happened.  After about an hour, I realised that nobody was actually resisting the change itself. They understood that lead-free solder was coming. They understood why. What they were resisting was the feeling that nobody understood how difficult the transition was for them.

For years they had built experience around the behaviours of leaded solder. They knew how it flowed. They knew how it looked. They knew what a good joint should feel and look like. Now, almost overnight, those visual and physical cues had changed. The solder looked dull. The finish was different. The process felt unfamiliar.

What management saw as resistance was actually uncertainty. What management saw as complaining was often professional concern. Once I understood that, the conversation changed completely.

We pulled in Process Engineering and Quality. Instead of arguing about the change, we started exploring the problems. What soldering tip would work best? Were temperature settings correct? Did we need more flux? Were operators applying the same techniques to a material that behaved differently?

Nothing about the requirement changed. We still had to move to lead-free solder. The standards didn't change. The timeline didn't change. What changed was our approach.

We stopped trying to force solutions before understanding the frustration. And once people felt understood, they became surprisingly willing to help find solutions.

**People Want Understanding Before Solutions**

This experience taught me a lesson that I have seen repeatedly throughout my career. Most people are not resisting the solution. They are resisting the feeling of not being understood. Managers often assume that if they explain the logic clearly enough, people will automatically support the decision.

But people do not operate purely on logic. They operate through a mixture of logic, experience, emotion, uncertainty, and fear.

Before people engage with your solution, they often want confidence that you understand their problem.

This is where Chris Voss's concept of **tactical empathy** becomes so powerful.[ ](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)[Never Split the Difference](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true) describes tactical empathy as recognising and vocalising another person's perspective without necessarily agreeing with it.[ ](https://gsi-my.sharepoint.com/personal/matthew_galea_novanta_com/_layouts/15/Doc.aspx?sourcedoc=%7B69BFD05C-F718-4D80-AA0D-9BAC543B8D0D%7D&file=Never%20Split%20the%20Difference.pptx&action=edit&mobileredirect=true)

The distinction is critical. Understanding somebody's concern is not the same as agreeing with it.

You can acknowledge frustration without changing the decision. You can recognise uncertainty without lowering standards. You can understand resistance without accepting poor behaviour.

### **Empathy Is Not Sympathy**

One of the biggest leadership mistakes I see is confusing empathy with sympathy. **Sympathy says**: *"You're right. This change is unfair." ***Empathy says**: *"It sounds like you're frustrated because the process no longer behaves the way you're used to."*

The difference may seem subtle, but the outcome is completely different. The first statement joins the complaint. The second statement demonstrates understanding. Chris Voss recommends using simple labels such as:

- "It sounds like..."
- "It seems like..."
- "It looks like..."
- "It appears that..."

The beauty of these statements is that they don't require agreement. They only require curiosity.

### **Understanding First, Accountability Second**

The most effective leaders I've worked with have understood something that many managers struggle with.  Empathy and accountability are not opposites. In fact, accountability becomes easier once people feel understood.

I could say to an operator: *"It sounds like you're frustrated that the solder behaves differently from what you've used for years." *And immediately follow it with: *"We still need to find a way to achieve the required quality standard."*

Both statements can exist at the same time. The first creates trust and the second creates accountability.

Without the first, the second feels like an order. With the first, the second feels like a problem we're solving together.

### **The Leadership Lesson**

Looking back, the lesson had very little to do with solder. It was about people. The quality team wanted to be understood. The operators wanted to be understood. Engineering wanted to be understood. Everyone was busy defending their position, and nobody felt heard.

The turning point wasn't a technical solution. It was taking the time to understand each perspective before trying to solve the problem. I've seen the same pattern during system implementations, process changes, performance discussions, supplier disputes, and project reviews.

People rarely need a manager to instantly solve their problem. More often, they need a manager willing to understand it. That doesn't mean avoiding difficult decisions. It doesn't mean lowering standards.

And it certainly doesn't mean becoming a pushover. It means recognising a simple truth: People are far more willing to move forward when they believe you're standing alongside them rather than pushing from behind.

And in my experience, that's where leadership becomes far more effective.
