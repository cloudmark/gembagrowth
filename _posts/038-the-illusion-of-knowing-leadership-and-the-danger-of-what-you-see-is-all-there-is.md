One of the quieter pressures of management is the expectation to know.

Not just to guide or support, but to have answers. To make decisions quickly. To speak with clarity, even when the situation itself is not entirely clear. Over time, that expectation shapes how we behave. We become more decisive, more confident, more willing to trust our judgment.

On the surface, these are all positive traits. But there is a risk underneath them.

I have been reflecting on this while reading Daniel Kahneman’s *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, particularly his discussion of the illusion of validity and the phrase “What You See Is All There Is”. The more I think about it, the more I recognise how easily these tendencies can take hold in leadership roles. If I am honest, I have been guilty of it myself.

The idea is simple but uncomfortable. We can feel confident in our judgments even when they are based on limited or unreliable information. Confidence may come not from strong evidence, but from coherence: if the story in our head makes sense, we tend to believe it is true.

We build conclusions from what is in front of us and behave as if it is the full picture. We rarely stop to consider what might be missing. In management, the picture is almost always incomplete.

### When a Convincing Impression Is Not Enough

One example Kahneman describes has stayed with me. Israeli military officers observed recruits during exercises and assessed their suitability for leadership roles. The structured observations gave the officers a strong sense that they could identify who would succeed.

When those assessments were later compared with how the recruits performed, the officers’ predictions proved to have limited predictive value. Yet the experience of observing the candidates had felt compelling. The story formed from what they saw seemed more certain than the evidence justified.

There is something familiar in that.

In the workplace, we do this constantly, just in more subtle ways. We meet someone once and feel we understand them. We leave an interview convinced a candidate will succeed. We see a team member in a handful of situations and form a lasting view of their capability.

Those judgments feel right. They feel complete.

But they are often built on fragments: a few interactions, a moment in time, limited context. From that, we create a full story without realising we are filling in the gaps.

Earlier in my career, I can see where I leaned into that confidence more than I should have. There is an unspoken belief that leadership means having clarity and that hesitation looks like weakness. So you form views quickly and trust your instinct.

Sometimes that works. But other times, what you are trusting is not deep insight. It is a well-constructed story built from incomplete information.

### Confidence Is Not the Same as Accuracy

What has changed for me is not a loss of confidence, but a different relationship with it. I am more aware that the feeling of certainty does not mean I am right. It often just means the explanation in my head hangs together well.

That is not the same as being accurate.

It has made me more comfortable pausing before drawing conclusions and asking whether I am reacting to evidence or to a narrative that happens to make sense. It also means acknowledging gaps: things I do not know, perspectives I have not heard, and data I have not yet seen.

That does not make decisions easier, but it makes them more grounded.

It also changes how I look back on decisions. With hindsight, everything looks obvious. Everyone becomes a genius after the fact. But we do not lead with hindsight. We lead with the information we have at the time.

Kierkegaard is often credited with capturing this idea: life can only be understood backwards, but it must be lived forwards.

We often associate strong leadership with decisiveness and certainty. But there is another, less visible side to it: being able to hold uncertainty without rushing to resolve it. Being willing to say, “We do not have enough information yet.”

That does not always feel comfortable. It does not always fit expectations. But in many cases, it is the more honest and disciplined response.

### Creating Better Checks

We cannot eliminate these biases simply by knowing about them. We are inclined to trust coherent stories and judgments that feel right. What we can do is build habits that make it easier to notice when confidence has outrun the evidence.

When a decision matters, we can:

- Separate what we directly observed from the interpretation we have added.
- Ask what evidence might challenge our current view, and what information is missing.
- Seek perspectives from people who have seen different parts of the situation.
- Use consistent criteria for decisions such as recruitment and performance assessment.
- Revisit the decision later and compare what we expected with what actually happened.

These practices do not guarantee a correct decision. They help make the reasoning more visible and reduce the chance that one convincing story becomes the whole picture.

For me, one of the most important shifts has been letting go of the need to appear as if I know everything. I can say, “The interview went well, but we will only know how the person performs once they are in the role.”

It sounds simple, but it challenges the ego. Our instinct is to say, “I knew it.” In reality, we rarely do—and that is fine. It is unrealistic to expect certainty in complex environments.

As Steve Jobs put it, we cannot connect the dots looking forward; we can only connect them looking backwards.

Leadership is not about knowing everything. It is about navigating uncertainty with honesty and clarity, and recognising that sometimes the biggest risk is not what we do not know.

It is believing, too quickly, that we already do.
