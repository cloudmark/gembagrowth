If I am honest, statistics was not something that came naturally to me. In fact, during my engineering undergraduate degree, it was quite the opposite. I struggled. I failed both mathematics and statistics. (Am not kidding here). At the time, the subject felt abstract, overly theoretical, and disconnected from anything real. Formulas were something to memorise, not something to understand.

Then something changed.

A couple of years later, while studying for my MBA, I took a module in qualitative and quantitative analysis. I expected more of the same. Instead, I experienced something completely different. Concepts that had once felt complex suddenly became intuitive. Standard deviation, variation, distributions, all of it started to make sense. Not because the maths had changed, but because the *context* had.  Credit must also be given to the lecturer that ignited this interest!.  He knows who he is!

For the first time, I could relate the concepts to real situations. I was no longer solving equations in isolation. I was understanding variation in processes, uncertainty in decisions, and patterns in behaviour. Statistics stopped being a subject and became a tool.

That shift was significant.

Working in manufacturing helped reinforce this even further. I began to see how simple statistical thinking could drive better decisions. A change in measurement was not always a real improvement. A small sample result did not define a process. Variation was not something to eliminate entirely, but something to understand and manage. These ideas are mathematically simple, but operationally powerful.

What struck me most over time was how widely these concepts apply.

Statistics is often associated with high-volume manufacturing or quality engineering. It is easy to assume it belongs in spreadsheets, dashboards, and technical reports. But in reality, it is everywhere.

Consider a fast food chain deciding how many burgers to prepare at different times of the day. That is demand forecasting and probability. A bank managing queues and wait times is dealing with variation and distribution. Schools use statistical models in admissions and performance analysis. Surveys that measure customer satisfaction rely on sampling and inference.

Beyond that, the reach is even broader.

Medicine uses statistics to evaluate treatments and assess risk. Psychology uses it to understand behaviour. Sports teams analyse performance data to gain competitive advantage. Travel companies optimise pricing (depends which side you look at this) based on demand patterns. Even everyday choices, such as when to leave the house to avoid traffic, are influenced by an implicit understanding of variability and probability.

The common thread is simple. Statistics helps us make sense of uncertainty.

Looking back, the turning point was not about becoming stronger at maths. It was about seeing relevance. Once I understood how these ideas applied to real problems, everything became more accessible. The formulas were no longer barriers, they were just expressions of concepts I already understood.

There is a wider lesson in this.

Many people are intimidated by statistics and data analysis. It carries a reputation for being difficult and overly technical. But often, the challenge is not the complexity of the subject. It is the lack of context. When applied to something familiar, the same concepts become far more approachable.

My experience is a reminder that understanding does not always come immediately. Sometimes it takes the right moment, the right context, or the right problem to bring everything into focus.

And when it does, what once felt difficult becomes not only manageable, but genuinely interesting.
