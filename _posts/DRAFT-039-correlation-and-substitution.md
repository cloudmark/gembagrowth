The order you ask questions can bias the answers you get.

We like to believe that good questions lead to objective answers. But what if the sequence of those questions is already influencing the outcome?

Correlation does not imply causation. We know that.
 What we rarely consider is this:

The way we ask questions can create the correlation itself.

This has real implications for how we run meetings, conduct performance reviews, and interpret data. Especially when we think we are being objective.

I have pulled together some thoughts on this, building on Kahneman’s work on substitution and decision making.

**When Questions Shape Answers: Substitution, Correlation, and the Influence of Sequence**

In management, we rely heavily on questions to understand what is really happening.

We ask them in day to day management, project reviews, interviews, performance discussions, team meetings, and surveys. There is an implicit assumption behind all of this that if we ask the right questions, we will get closer to the truth. It feels straightforward. Ask, listen, decide.

But there is a complication. The way we ask questions can influence the answers we receive, sometimes more than we realise. Logic suggests that a question should have a consistent answer, regardless of how it is asked. In practice, that is not always the case.

This stood out to me while reading *Thinking, Fast and Slow* by Daniel Kahneman. It builds on the idea that our thinking is less objective than we assume. Two concepts are particularly relevant here: substitution and sequence.

Kahneman explains that when we are faced with a difficult question, our mind often answers a simpler one instead. This happens automatically, without conscious awareness. The answer feels valid, but it may not address the original question.

He illustrates this with a well known study involving students. They were asked two questions: how happy they were with their lives, and how many dates they had been on in the past month.

When asked in that order, the correlation between dating frequency and happiness was low. In other words, how much someone was dating did not appear to be closely linked to how happy they were overall.

However, when the order was reversed, the result changed. When students were first asked about their dating life, and then about their overall happiness, the correlation became significantly stronger. Happiness now appeared closely tied to dating activity.

Nothing about their lives had changed. Only the sequence of the questions had.

What changed was attention. By asking about dating first, it became more immediate and available in the mind. When answering the broader question about life satisfaction, participants substituted it with a simpler, more accessible one: how they felt about their dating life at that moment.

The answer felt right, but it was partly answering a different question.

There is something important in that. We often say that correlation does not imply causation. That remains true. But this example highlights something else. Correlation itself can be influenced by how questions are framed and, in this case, the order in which they are asked.

What looks like a meaningful relationship between variables may partly reflect how the data was gathered.

That has clear implications in a management setting. Much of what we rely on comes from structured conversations, yet those conversations are rarely neutral. They are shaped by what is brought to attention first.

Take performance reviews. If the discussion begins with a recent issue or mistake, that example becomes prominent. When the broader question of overall performance is discussed, it is difficult not to let that recent event influence the judgement.

This is not deliberate bias. It is simply the mind using what is most available.

The same applies in meetings. The first topic raised often anchors the discussion. It frames how everything that follows is interpreted. By the end, the conclusion can feel obvious, when in reality it has been influenced by where the conversation began.

Reflecting on this, I find myself questioning how often I assume that answers are independent and objective. In practice, they are often shaped by context and sequence in ways that are easy to overlook.

It also changes how I think about data. When we observe a strong relationship between two factors, our instinct is to treat it as meaningful. We rarely stop to consider whether that relationship may have been influenced by how the information was collected or presented.

What I take from this is not that we should distrust every answer, but that we should be more aware of how context shapes outcomes. Sometimes the real question is not just what answer we received, but what led to it.

This has made me more deliberate in how I structure conversations when it matters. Not in a rigid way, but with an awareness that order has consequences. What is discussed first creates a ripple effect on everything that follows.

There are also practical implications. If a difficult discussion has taken place today, asking for general feedback the next day may introduce bias. Recency will influence the response. In some cases, it may be better to acknowledge this directly or allow time before revisiting broader questions.

More generally, we can call out situations where sequence may distort perspective. Simply recognising it can reduce its effect.

There is also a broader point about leadership.

We often see decisions as responses to reality. In practice, leaders also shape that reality through how they frame discussions and what they bring into focus. The influence is subtle, but it is real.

Kahneman’s work does not suggest we can eliminate these effects. They are part of how we think. We will always be influenced by what is most immediate and accessible.

What we can do is recognise it. We can be more thoughtful in how we ask questions and more reflective in how we interpret answers. When conclusions appear clear or correlations appear strong, we can pause and consider whether they have been shaped by context.

In the end, it comes back to a simple idea.

We like to believe we are observing reality clearly. In practice, we are often shaping it at the same time.

And sometimes, the difference between insight and illusion comes down to the order in which the questions were asked.
