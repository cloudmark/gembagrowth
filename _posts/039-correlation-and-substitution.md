We like to believe that good questions lead to objective answers. **But what if the order of those questions already shapes the outcome?**

In management, we rely on questions to understand what is really happening—in meetings, reviews, interviews, and surveys. The assumption is simple: ask, listen, decide. Yet the way we ask, and what we ask first, can influence the answers we receive more than we realise.

In *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Daniel Kahneman describes **substitution**: when faced with a difficult question, our minds sometimes answer an easier one instead, without noticing. The answer feels relevant, but it may not address the original question. The context around a question can influence which easier question comes to mind.

### A Question of Order

A well-known study by psychologists Norbert Schwarz and colleagues illustrates how question order can matter. Students were asked to rate how happy they were with their lives and how many dates they had been on in the past month.

When students answered the life-satisfaction question first, their answers showed little relationship with the number of dates they reported. But when they were asked about dates first, the two answers were more strongly associated.

The students’ circumstances had not changed between questions. What changed was the context. Thinking about dating first may have made it more salient when students considered their broader life satisfaction. The abstract question “How happy am I with my life?” may, for some people, have been answered using a more immediate cue: “How is my dating life going?”

This is an example of how a relationship measured in answers can depend on the way information is gathered. It does not mean that question order changed people’s actual happiness, or that dating causes happiness. It shows that the answers we use to measure those ideas can be influenced by context and sequence.

### The Same Effect at Work

In management, the same pattern can appear in ordinary conversations.

Begin a performance review by discussing a recent mistake, and that event may colour how both people interpret the rest of the conversation. Start a meeting with a contentious issue, and it can anchor how later points are received. The first topic can shape what feels important and which examples come readily to mind.

This is not necessarily deliberate bias. It is one way our minds use what is most available. But it reminds us that answers are not always independent of context. What people say may reflect the question itself, the timing, and what they have just been asked to consider.

### Better Questions, Better Decisions

When we see a strong relationship between two factors, it is tempting to jump straight to an explanation. But correlation by itself does not tell us what caused what. The relationship might be influenced by other factors, by the way the data was collected, or by the framing of the questions.

The same caution applies in conversations: before treating an answer as a complete account, consider what might have shaped it. Was the question leading? Was a recent event made especially salient? Did the order of topics create an impression that would have been different if the conversation had started elsewhere?

We cannot remove every influence from how people think and respond. We can be more deliberate:

- Use neutral wording and ask the same core questions when comparing people or situations.
- Consider whether the order of questions could influence the answers.
- Separate what someone directly observed from the interpretation they draw from it.
- Ask open follow-up questions, such as “What else might explain this?”
- Avoid treating a correlation as proof of cause without further evidence.

For leaders, this is not about distrusting every answer. It is about recognising that the way we frame a conversation can shape what comes into focus. When the decision matters, slowing down to examine that context can help us hear more than the first, most available explanation.

We like to believe we observe reality clearly. In truth, we are often shaping what we notice at the same time. Sometimes, the difference between a useful insight and an incomplete story comes down to the questions we ask—and the order in which we ask them.
