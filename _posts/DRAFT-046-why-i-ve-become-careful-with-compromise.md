One of the earliest leadership lessons I learned sounded sensible at the time: *"When people disagree, meet in the middle."*

It's advice most of us hear throughout our careers. When two departments have competing priorities, split the difference. When two colleagues disagree, find a compromise. When stakeholders want different outcomes, meet halfway.

On the surface, it feels like good leadership. It feels balanced, fair, and pragmatic.

The problem is that after years of working with teams, projects, suppliers, customers, and internal stakeholders, I've started to realise that compromise is not always the virtue we make it out to be.

We often talk about "meeting in the middle", as if the middle automatically represents the best outcome.

But meeting on a bridge doesn't necessarily mean meeting in the middle of the bridge.

Sometimes the smartest thing to do is walk all the way across because that's where the best solution sits.

One of my favourite illustrations of this idea is the example of a husband who wants to wear brown shoes and a wife who wants him to wear black shoes. If the objective is simply to reach a compromise, the answer becomes absurd. One black shoe and one brown shoe.

Nobody gets what they want.  More importantly, nobody gets a good outcome.  It's an amusing example, written by Chris Voss in his book, Never split the difference.  It captures a problem that appears in organisations every day. We become so focused on ending the disagreement that we stop focusing on solving the problem.

### **The Difference Between Compromise and Win-Win**

Stephen Covey's principle of *Think Win-Win* has always resonated with me, particularly because it is often misunderstood.  Many people assume Win-Win means compromise.  I don't believe it does.

Compromise often means both parties give something up.  Win-Win means both parties gain something meaningful.  Those are very different outcomes.

Imagine a sales team pushing for shorter lead times while operations push for schedule stability. A compromise might involve reducing lead times slightly while preserving most of the existing planning process.

Everyone gets a little of what they wanted.  Nobody gets enough of what they needed.  Sales remain frustrated because customers still aren't receiving the responsiveness they expect. Operations remain frustrated because the disruption hasn't reduced. The organisation congratulates itself for finding middle ground while the original problem remains largely unchanged.

A true Win-Win conversation starts somewhere different.

Instead of asking, *"Where can we meet in the middle?"*, it asks:  *"What are each of us actually trying to achieve?"  *That question changes everything.  Because the sales team may not really want shorter lead times.

They may want improved customer satisfaction.  Operations may not really want rigid schedules.  They may want predictable and efficient production.

Once you understand the interests behind the positions, entirely new possibilities begin to emerge.

### **The Best Solutions Often Sit Somewhere Else**

One of the patterns I've noticed throughout my career is that people tend to arrive at discussions carrying solutions rather than problems.  They arrive convinced they know what needs to happen.  We need more overtime.  We need another planner.  We need additional inventory.  We need more maintenance time.

But those are often proposed solutions, not the underlying need.  The real requirement may be capacity, service, reliability, responsiveness, or stability.

When leaders focus exclusively on the proposed solution, the conversation quickly becomes a debate between competing positions. Eventually someone suggests meeting halfway and everybody reluctantly agrees.

When leaders focus on the underlying objective, however, the discussion becomes much more interesting.

You're no longer choosing between Option A and Option B.  You're exploring Option C, D, and E, solutions that nobody had initially considered because they were too busy defending their own position.

I've seen this happen countless times in manufacturing environments.  A production team and a maintenance team can appear to be pulling in opposite directions. One wants machines running. The other wants machines stopped for preventative maintenance.

At first glance, compromise seems inevitable.  Yet when you dig deeper, both groups usually want exactly the same thing: reliable output.

Once that shared objective becomes clear, the discussion changes completely. Instead of negotiating over downtime, the conversation moves towards reliability, predictive maintenance, scheduling, condition monitoring, or sequencing.

The disagreement wasn't really about maintenance hours at all.  It was about achieving a common goal.

### **Why Leaders Rush Towards Compromise**

I think managers are often drawn to compromise because it creates the appearance of progress.  Disagreement is uncomfortable. It slows meetings down.  It creates tension.  Compromise offers a quick escape route.

Everybody gives something up, everybody shakes hands, and the meeting ends.  The challenge is that unresolved issues don't disappear simply because an agreement has been reached.

They usually resurface later, often in the form of poorer performance, workarounds, frustration, or missed objectives.

I've seen change initiatives where a proposed solution was gradually watered down to gain acceptance from everybody involved. What began as a meaningful improvement eventually became a diluted version of itself.

Resistance decreased.  The benefits disappeared with it.  The organisation succeeded in avoiding conflict but failed to solve the original problem.  That experience taught me something important:  An agreement is not automatically a good outcome.  The quality of the agreement matters.

### **A Better Question**

These days, when I'm faced with competing viewpoints, I try to resist the temptation to ask where the middle ground lies.

Instead, I try to understand what success looks like for each side.  What are we actually trying to achieve?  What concern sits behind this position?  What risk are we trying to avoid?  What outcome would genuinely create value?  Those questions often reveal that people are far more aligned than they first appear.  The disagreement is usually about how to get there, not where they want to go.  And once that becomes clear, compromise often stops being necessary.

As a younger manager, I believed one of my responsibilities was helping people find middle ground.  Today, I think my responsibility is helping people find common ground.  They're not the same thing.  Middle ground is where opposing positions meet.  Common ground is where shared interests begin.  One often produces compromise.  The other creates the possibility of a genuine Win-Win outcome.

Because sometimes the answer sits in the middle.But more often, the best answer is somewhere neither side was looking when the conversation began.  The answer might sit on a different bridge!
